# Flight and immersion history

**First Navette flight surfaces**

- The film outlet used a 0.34 × 0.38 m ellipse. Its plume lit a small area at the back of each curved grille. The first full-aperture pass made all five streams visible but overlit the edge where the grille turned tangent to the exhaust. The fitted version weights that edge by the projected area of the slots.

- The initial flight variant slid and scaled generic airfoils under the nacelles. At 430 m/s near sea level the actuator settled at 28% and the outboard wing remained inside its casing.
- The film packet carried the original crew and its glazing had a separate dry shader path. The flight variant switched to nacelle pivots and a fitted weather chart.
- The first fitted wing spanned 7.80 m and sat above the turbine on two small covers. The profile review led to wider molded shoulders and a lower hinge. A second teal stripe and a concentrated plume were discarded after side and aft flight views.

- The next version used 86-degree hinges and a 9.36 m span. In the pilot's rear-quarter view the independent foil still crossed the nacelle lip. Pulling the fin root forward by height also put a step in the afterdeck. Its source base is tagged `piste/navette-profil-2026-11-21-base`.

**First assisted brake**

- The previous cruise controller reduced speed with a 0.65 s response. The holding brake removed speed with a 0.625 s response and a powered hold depended on keeping X pressed.
- Pilot feedback called for smoother braking, visible control surfaces and automatic stabilization after the stop.

**Exterior shock-layer study**

- The local tag `piste/rentree-volumetrique-2026-11-20-base` identifies the preceding surface-based flight code. A hot view and a fast, level view showed no warm change in the sampled air ahead of the nose or beside the hull.
- The first volume surrounded the craft in pursuit, but a direct line of sight through the gas retained only 12% of the cockpit horizon contrast. This made the flight deck look submerged in an opaque pink fog.
- Reducing only the bulk density recovered the horizon but left two isolated bright patches ahead of the bow. An advected layer with a thin continuous front and longitudinal filaments gave them a common flow around the ship.

**Pilot review of continuous thrust and continuous heating**

- The second drive gave 4,845 m/s in two seconds over the sea at full power. Its fifth wheel notch gave only 0.77 m/s after a second. The pilot reported sluggish acceleration on the wheel and excessive lateral drift after steering.
- In dense air the unpowered hull slowed from 5,000 to 2,934 m/s in a second. Steering and wheel changes offered little guidance towards a new flight path.
- Heat originally followed density and airspeed for the entire flight. A 7,000 m/s descent from 70 km reached heat 0.43 after five seconds, but a fast low pass could also keep the effect active. The pilot requested an entry event with a clear end.

**First surface-attached trial**

- Seven six-second films covered the three hot cockpits, three dry grazing-sun views and one slow approach. At 7,100 m/s, 80% of port and 38% of starboard gasket samples changed by at least 25 levels out of 255. A central sky patch stayed within 12 levels. These criteria measured light on the glass and missed the lack of a shock layer around the hull.
- The pilot requested a luminous volume and visible grains around the ship after reviewing the seal-based treatment. A matched native exterior view of that build is retained locally under `artifacts/vol-assiste-2026-11-20/avant/`.
- The first mounted gas sheet lit the real seal. During a sideways glance, straight cuts interrupted broad peach-coloured bands. A flat-colour draw covered the complete aperture. Two pixels on opposite sides of a cut were on the same packed triangle.
- Long luminous streaks read as rods. Later particle tuning bounds the optical streak length at the pilot's eye. The retainer reflection accounts for material roughness, albedo and distance to the seal.
- The initial groove clusters produced three bright parallel marks below the horizon in the grazing-sun shot. Later cuts vary in length and curvature. Both loss of transmitted light and directional reflection contribute to their visibility.
